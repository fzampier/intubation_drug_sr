% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================================
\section*{Abstract}

\textbf{Background:}
Etomidate and ketamine are the most commonly used induction agents for emergency tracheal intubation in critically ill adults. However, comparative evidence regarding their effects on mortality and hemodynamic outcomes remains uncertain.

\textbf{Methods:}
We searched MEDLINE and Embase (inception to [Month 2026]) for randomized controlled trials comparing etomidate, ketamine, propofol, or ketamine--propofol combination (ketofol) for emergency or rapid sequence intubation in critically ill adults. We performed random-effects network meta-analysis using the frequentist framework. The primary outcome was short-term mortality (28--30 day or in-hospital). Secondary outcomes included cardiovascular collapse, post-induction hypotension, vasopressor use, first-pass intubation success, and peri-intubation cardiac arrest. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the CINeMA framework.

\textbf{Results:}
Ten trials (4,752 patients, four treatments) were included. For the primary outcome, there was no difference between ketamine and etomidate (OR 0.97, 95\% CI 0.81--1.16; $I^2$ = 25\%; moderate certainty). Evidence for other mortality comparisons was sparse: ketamine vs propofol (OR 1.53, 0.80--2.92; 1 trial; low certainty) and etomidate vs propofol (OR 0.63, 0.32--1.24; indirect only; very low certainty). Compared with etomidate, ketamine was associated with higher odds of cardiovascular collapse (OR 1.44, 1.20--1.71; moderate certainty), post-induction hypotension (OR 1.32, 1.05--1.65; low certainty), and peri-intubation vasopressor use (OR 1.43, 1.16--1.77; low certainty). However, ketamine was associated with lower extended vasopressor use (OR 0.78, 0.62--0.97; low certainty). No differences were observed for first-pass intubation success or cardiac arrest.

\textbf{Conclusions:}
Etomidate and ketamine probably result in similar short-term mortality when used for emergency intubation in critically ill adults. Ketamine may increase peri-intubation hemodynamic instability, while etomidate may increase delayed vasopressor requirements, possibly reflecting adrenal suppression.

\textbf{Keywords:} intubation; rapid sequence induction; etomidate; ketamine; propofol; network meta-analysis; critically ill

\section{Methods}

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251251225) and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA-NMA extension.\cite{hutton2015}

\subsection{Eligibility criteria}

We included parallel-group randomized controlled trials comparing etomidate, ketamine (including esketamine or S-ketamine), propofol, or ketamine--propofol combination (ketofol) as induction agents for emergency or rapid sequence intubation in critically ill or acutely ill adults ($\geq$16 years). Studies were eligible regardless of clinical setting (emergency department, intensive care unit, prehospital, or acute hospital ward). Co-interventions (opioids, neuromuscular blocking agents, preoxygenation strategies) were permitted if applied similarly across treatment arms.

We excluded observational studies, crossover trials, simulation or volunteer studies, conference abstracts without sufficient data for risk of bias assessment and effect estimation, studies of elective or operating-room intubations, procedural sedation without intubation, and studies where the randomized intervention was not the hypnotic induction agent. Only English-language publications were included.

\subsection{Information sources and search strategy}

We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Embase (via Ovid) from inception to [Month 2026]. Forward citation searching of included trials was performed to identify additional relevant studies. Trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP) were consulted for contextual information. The complete search strategies are provided in the Supplement.

\subsection{Risk of bias assessment}

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool\cite{sterne2019} across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each study was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias.

\subsection{Outcomes}

The primary outcome was short-term mortality, defined as 28--30 day mortality where reported, or in-hospital mortality when 28--30 day data were unavailable. Secondary outcomes included: cardiovascular collapse (composite of hypotension, vasopressor initiation or escalation, and/or cardiac arrest, as defined by individual studies); post-induction hypotension (blood pressure below study-defined thresholds within 0--15 minutes); new or escalated vasopressor use (analyzed at peri-intubation, 24-hour, and extended time points separately); first-pass intubation success; and peri-intubation cardiac arrest. All binary outcomes were analyzed as odds ratios (OR) with 95\% confidence intervals (CI).

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Arm-level binary data were converted to study-level contrasts (log OR and standard error) using the \texttt{pairwise()} function from the \textit{meta} package (version 8.2-1).\cite{balduzzi2019} Random-effects network meta-analysis was performed using the frequentist approach implemented in the \textit{netmeta} package (version 3.3-1)\cite{rucker2020} in R (version 4.5.2). Etomidate was designated as the reference treatment. Esketamine and S-ketamine were grouped under the ketamine node.

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using $I^2$ and $\tau^2$. Global inconsistency was evaluated using the design-by-treatment interaction test, and local inconsistency was assessed through node-splitting. Treatment rankings were summarized using P-scores.\cite{rucker2015}

Pairwise meta-analysis of etomidate versus ketamine was performed using the Paule--Mandel estimator for $\tau^2$. 95\% prediction intervals were calculated where appropriate.

\subsection{Subgroup and sensitivity analyses}

Pre-specified subgroup analyses for the primary outcome included stratification by clinical setting (emergency department, ICU, or mixed) and overall risk of bias. A planned subgroup analysis by baseline vasopressor use was not feasible because only three of ten studies reported this information.

Sensitivity analyses excluded: (a) studies where the ketamine arm included a midazolam adjunct (Cinar 2011,\cite{cinar2011} Dormans 2014\cite{dormans2014}); (b) high risk-of-bias studies (Dormans 2014\cite{dormans2014}); (c) small studies ($<$30 patients per arm); and (d) analyses collapsing or removing ketofol as a separate treatment node.

\subsection{Certainty of evidence}

Certainty of evidence for key comparisons was assessed using the CINeMA framework (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis),\cite{nikolakopoulou2020} evaluating six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Ratings were classified as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Study selection}

% TODO: describe PRISMA flow
The search identified [N] records after deduplication. After title/abstract screening and full-text review, 10 trials met the inclusion criteria (Figure~\ref{fig:prisma}).

\subsection{Study characteristics}

The 10 included trials\cite{jabre2009,cinar2011,dormans2014,smischney2019,matchett2022,knack2023,srivilaithon2023,casey2025,agarwal2025,schmidt2025} enrolled 4,752 patients across four treatment nodes: etomidate (9 trials), ketamine (9 trials), propofol (1 trial), and ketofol (1 trial). Studies were conducted in ICU (5 trials), emergency department (4 trials), and mixed settings (2 trials), across seven countries. Sample sizes ranged from 22 to 2,359 patients. The characteristics of included studies are presented in Table~\ref{tab:characteristics}.

\subsection{Risk of bias}

Nine studies were rated as having ``some concerns'' and one (Dormans 2014\cite{dormans2014}) as ``high risk'' of bias (Figure~\ref{fig:rob}). The most common concerns were lack of blinding (open-label designs in 7/10 studies) and deviations from intended interventions. Mortality, as an objective outcome, was considered less susceptible to bias from lack of blinding.

\subsection{Primary outcome: short-term mortality}

Ten trials contributed to the mortality network meta-analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:main}A). Network heterogeneity was low ($I^2$ = 25\%, $\tau^2$ = 0.015). There was no evidence of global inconsistency (design-by-treatment interaction test, $p$ = [X.XX]).

Ketamine and etomidate resulted in similar short-term mortality (OR 0.97, 95\% CI 0.81--1.16; 8 direct studies, 4,425 patients; moderate certainty) (Figure~\ref{fig:main}B, Table~\ref{tab:results}). The comparison of ketamine vs propofol was based on a single trial and showed a non-significant trend toward higher mortality with ketamine (OR 1.53, 95\% CI 0.80--2.92; low certainty). The indirect estimate for etomidate vs propofol suggested potentially lower mortality with propofol (OR 0.63, 95\% CI 0.32--1.24; very low certainty). No mortality difference was observed between etomidate and ketofol (OR 0.84, 95\% CI 0.41--1.71; 1 trial; low certainty).

All results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results}.

\subsection{Secondary outcomes}

Compared with etomidate, ketamine was associated with significantly higher odds of cardiovascular collapse (OR 1.44, 95\% CI 1.20--1.71; moderate certainty), post-induction hypotension (OR 1.32, 95\% CI 1.05--1.65; low certainty), and peri-intubation vasopressor use (OR 1.43, 95\% CI 1.16--1.77; low certainty). Vasopressor use at 24 hours showed substantial heterogeneity ($I^2$ = 93\%) and was not reliably estimable.

In contrast, ketamine was associated with lower extended vasopressor requirements (OR 0.78, 95\% CI 0.62--0.97; low certainty), with the reversal in direction potentially reflecting etomidate-induced adrenal suppression.

No differences were observed for first-pass intubation success (OR 0.94, 95\% CI 0.77--1.15; moderate certainty) or peri-intubation cardiac arrest (OR 0.97, 95\% CI 0.52--1.81; low certainty).

\subsection{Subgroup and sensitivity analyses}

The effect of etomidate versus ketamine on mortality was consistent across clinical settings (ED, ICU, mixed; test for subgroup differences $p$ = 0.77) and across risk-of-bias categories ($p$ = 0.94). Excluding studies with midazolam adjuncts, high risk of bias, or small sample sizes did not materially change the results. Collapsing ketofol into the ketamine node (OR 0.96, 0.82--1.12) or removing the ketofol node entirely (OR 0.97, 0.81--1.16) yielded similar estimates (eFigures in the Supplement).

\subsection{Certainty of evidence}

Certainty was moderate for the etomidate--ketamine mortality comparison (downgraded for within-study bias) and for cardiovascular collapse and first-pass success. Certainty was low or very low for all other comparisons and outcomes, primarily due to imprecision, indirectness (especially for comparisons involving propofol, which relied on a single trial or indirect evidence), and heterogeneous outcome definitions (Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\section{Conclusions}

Etomidate and ketamine probably result in similar short-term mortality when used as induction agents for emergency tracheal intubation in critically ill adults (moderate certainty). Ketamine is associated with higher peri-intubation hemodynamic instability, including cardiovascular collapse and vasopressor escalation. However, etomidate may increase delayed vasopressor requirements, possibly reflecting adrenal suppression. Evidence comparing either agent with propofol remains very limited. These findings support the use of either etomidate or ketamine, with the choice guided by clinical context and anticipated hemodynamic trajectory.
